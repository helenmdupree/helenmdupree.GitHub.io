You Don’t Transform Revenue by Hiring for Yesterday’s Job

Commercial transformation requires roles, resources, expectations, and measures of success built for the future revenue model.

By Helen M. Dupree · Founder / Principal, SOUBEL

Organizations often say they want growth when what they really need is transformation. Those are not the same thing.

When the revenue model is changing, the commercial role responsible for delivering it may need to change with it. Yet companies often start with a traditional account-management job description and then expect the person they hire to become a market strategist, new-business hunter, commercialization leader, relationship builder, cross-functional connector, and change agent.

That is not an expanded version of the same job. It is a different job.

A future-facing commercial role should be built around the outcome the business actually wants to create. If the goal is expansion, the person in that role has to be able to find whitespace inside existing accounts, recognize new buying triggers, uncover under-commercialized capabilities, and develop opportunities the organization may not be pursuing today.

If the goal is transformation, that person also needs room to examine how the commercial model works and where it may need to evolve. Not because everything that came before was wrong, but because tomorrow’s revenue will not automatically come from yesterday’s structure.

Leadership has a role in this too. Someone hired to create a different result needs access to information, visibility across functions, support from technical and operational teams, and a clear understanding of how success will be measured.

Most importantly, the organization has to decide what it is actually hiring for.

Is the expectation to protect existing relationships, expand strategic accounts, or build a new market? Is it to commercialize technical capabilities, build relevance with executive buyers, or develop a different route to revenue?

Those objectives may overlap, but they call for different behaviors, different skills, and often different measures of success. The mistake is assuming one traditional role can quietly absorb all of them without changing the environment around it.

That is how an organization ends up hiring for transformation while continuing to manage for maintenance.

And that is an expensive contradiction.

Commercial roles should be designed around the future revenue model, not simply inherited responsibilities. Sometimes the greatest commercial mistake is not hiring the wrong person… it is hiring the right person for the wrong job.

If the mandate is transformation, then the role, resources, expectations, and measures of success have to be built for transformation too.

Tomorrow’s revenue cannot be created inside yesterday’s job description.
