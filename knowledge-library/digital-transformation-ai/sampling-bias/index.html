SAMPLING BIAS

A model can learn where the organization looked, not only what the asset did.

Historical inspection and integrity data are often collected according to risk, access, regulation, known problems, technology availability, and operating priorities. Those choices shape the dataset before machine learning ever begins.

Why sampling mattersBack to Digital & AI Knowledge

WHY IT MATTERS

More observations do not always mean more representative evidence.

Industrial datasets are rarely assembled under laboratory conditions. Operators may inspect areas with known corrosion history more frequently than areas with no prior indication. Certain assets may have richer sensor histories because they were easier to instrument. Older segments may carry different record quality than newer ones. Some locations may be excavated precisely because other evidence already suggested concern.

When those records become training data, the collection strategy can become part of what the model learns. A dataset with many findings in heavily inspected areas may reflect genuine degradation, heavier scrutiny, or both. Conversely, lightly inspected areas can appear quiet because little evidence exists, not because the underlying condition is necessarily better.

The SOUBEL research inventory identified this as a question worth explicit scrutiny. It did not establish a new pipeline-specific theory of bias, and it did not prove that any particular model is biased. The practical point is more modest and more useful: before relying on historical data for prediction, organizations should ask how the data were collected and whether the sampling pattern affects what conclusions are justified.

PRACTICAL QUESTIONS

What should be understood about the dataset before training or trusting a model?

Why was this location inspected? Was it scheduled routinely, selected by regulation, chosen because of prior concern, or triggered by another signal?
Where is coverage sparse? Which assets, locations, operating states, or time periods are underrepresented?
Has the inspection method changed? Different tools, thresholds, procedures, or technologies can alter what is detectable and comparable.
Are findings and non-findings represented equally well? A dataset centered on defects may not describe the broader asset population.
Could missing data be informative? Absence of records may reflect low concern, limited access, legacy systems, or incomplete migration rather than healthy condition.
How will the model be validated? Is performance tested on evidence independent enough to reveal whether the model generalizes beyond the historical sampling pattern?

SOUBEL practical interpretation: These are review questions derived from the research inventory and broader ML-bias concepts. They are not presented as a formal statistical correction method.

RESEARCH FOUNDATION

The research review treated representativeness as an open industrial-AI question.

The research inventory noted that corrosion and digital-twin literature increasingly discusses data quality and trust, while historical inspection data are often non-random. It also identified broader NIST/IEEE work on machine-learning bias as relevant background and flagged PHMSA integrity-management studies on inspection planning for deeper review.

Because the academic novelty search was inconclusive, this page does not claim that sampling bias in pipeline integrity is newly discovered or uniquely defined by SOUBEL. It presents the issue as a practical reason to examine how inspection strategy, missingness, and coverage can influence the evidence available to AI.

Reference foundation: corrosion/ML and digital-twin literature inventoried by SOUBEL · broader NIST/IEEE ML-bias literature identified in the research review · pipeline integrity inspection-planning literature identified for continued study.

THE DECISION TEST

Does the model describe the asset population, or mainly the history of where evidence was collected?

That question belongs in model validation whenever historical inspection patterns shape the training data.
