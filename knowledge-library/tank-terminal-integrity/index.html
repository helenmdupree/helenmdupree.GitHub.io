OPERATING CONTEXT

Integrity depends on how storage, transfer, containment, and response work together.

Receipt, storage, transfer, loading, and containment rely on connected equipment, controls, and people. When conditions change in one part of the operation, the effect can carry into another.

Storage interface

Tank bottom, shell, roof, foundation, product, water, coatings, corrosion control, nozzles, seals, and appurtenances.

Transfer interface

Piping, pumps, valves, manifolds, meters, loading systems, buried connections, supports, and mechanical equipment.

Control interface

Level measurement, alarms, overfill prevention, interlocks, shutdown functions, monitoring, and operating procedures.

Execution interface

Inspection, engineering, operations, maintenance, contractors, repair, verification, records, and return-to-service decisions.

STORAGE & TRANSFER INTEGRITY

The integrity boundary extends beyond the tank.

Tanks, piping, pumps, valves, manifolds, loading systems, and connected controls operate as one product-handling system. Condition, corrosion, service history, mechanical reliability, and isolation capability have to be understood together.

TANK CONDITION

Bottom, shell, roof, foundation, and appurtenances tell different parts of the story.

Thickness history, settlement, coatings and linings, cathodic protection, nozzles, seals, drainage, repairs, and operating service all influence the integrity picture.

TRANSFER PIPING

Buried and aboveground sections face different threats.

Dead legs, low points, supports, coatings, CP where applicable, internal service, atmospheric exposure, modifications, and operating changes shape inspection and maintenance decisions.

MECHANICAL & LOADING SYSTEMS

Operability and containment are part of integrity.

Pumps, valves, meters, loading arms, hoses, couplings, isolation capability, vibration, leakage, and maintenance history affect both reliability and response.

CORROSION, INSPECTION & CONDITION EVIDENCE

The evidence has to match the mechanism and the question.

Terminal facilities combine soil, atmosphere, product, water, coatings, linings, insulation, concrete, and electrical systems. Inspection becomes useful when the method, exposure, corrosion mechanism, and operating context remain connected.

CORROSION ENVIRONMENT

Exposure changes across the facility.

Tank bottoms, shells, roofs, buried piping, aboveground piping, supports, water traps, coatings, linings, insulation, and electrical interfaces can create very different corrosion conditions.

INSPECTION & MEASUREMENT

Use the method that can answer the integrity question.

Visual inspection, UT, floor scanning, weld and leak testing, dimensional surveys, settlement evaluation, remote inspection, and field measurements each provide different evidence with different limitations.

PROTECTION & VERIFICATION

Protection systems need proof they are performing as intended.

Coatings, linings, cathodic protection, monitoring, field measurements, thickness history, excavation or repair findings, and post-work verification strengthen the decision record.

ENGINEERING INTERPRETATION

Condition evidence becomes decision support through qualified judgment.

Remaining life, fitness-for-service, risk, repair planning, return-to-service decisions, and reassessment depend on material condition, loads, service, consequence, uncertainty, and governing criteria.

CONTAINMENT, OVERFILL & RESPONSE

Prevention depends on equipment, controls, timing, and ownership working together.

Release prevention is not one device or one procedure. It is the combination of reliable level information, independent protection, operating limits, transfer discipline, containment condition, isolation capability, and a prepared response path.

OVERFILL PREVENTION

Information has to lead to prepared action.

Tank capacity, operating limits, level measurement, alarms, shutdown actions, transfer rates, testing, maintenance, response time, and accountability form the protection system.

SECONDARY CONTAINMENT

Containment condition is part of facility integrity.

Dikes, berms, floors, drainage controls, penetrations, valves, accumulated water, inspection findings, and maintenance affect how effectively the facility limits consequence.

RELEASE RESPONSE

The response begins before the event.

Isolation, shutdown capability, communications, spill planning, access, procedures, maintenance condition, and field readiness determine how quickly a facility can control and learn from a release.

LIFECYCLE & DECISION CONTINUITY

The next integrity decision depends on what the facility remembers.

Design basis, inspection history, corrosion rates, repairs, operating service, alarms, modifications, field findings, and verification become more valuable when the organization can connect them across time and across disciplines.
