PIPELINE INTEGRITY

Follow the evidence
through the integrity cycle.

Pipeline integrity is the ability of a pipeline system to remain fit for its intended service and perform safely, reliably, and effectively under the operating and environmental conditions it is expected to encounter.

Start with the fundamentalsReturn to Expertise

FOUNDATION

What pipeline integrity means.

Maintaining integrity requires understanding what can threaten the pipeline, evaluating what is known about its condition, selecting assessment methods appropriate to the threat, acting on the evidence, and using what is learned to improve the next decision.

What can threaten pipeline integrity?

Pipeline integrity can be affected by multiple threat types, and more than one may be present at the same time.

Corrosion and material degradation can include external corrosion, internal corrosion, coating deterioration, stress corrosion cracking, and other forms of material loss or degradation.

Material, manufacturing, and construction-related conditions can include defects associated with pipe materials, seams, welds, joints, fabrication, installation, or construction.

Third-party and outside-force damage can include excavation damage, dents, gouges, impact, ground movement, geohazards, flooding, erosion, and other external loads or environmental forces.

Operational and equipment-related conditions can include equipment or component failure, abnormal pressure or flow conditions, control-system problems, valve or regulator malfunction, incorrect operation, and operating changes that place the pipeline outside its normal or intended service conditions.

Aging, service, and performance conditions can include leakage, loss of capacity, structural deterioration, declining service performance, and other age-related conditions that reduce the pipeline’s ability to perform as intended.

How pipeline integrity is maintained.

Maintaining pipeline integrity is ongoing work. It includes preventing or controlling threats where possible, assessing pipeline condition using methods appropriate to the threat, analyzing the results with other relevant information, addressing conditions that may reduce integrity, applying preventive or mitigative measures, and reassessing the pipeline as conditions and information change.

Pipeline integrity describes the condition and capability of the pipeline; integrity management is the ongoing process used to preserve it over time.

Reference framework: This overview reflects common integrity principles across energy and water pipeline systems. Regulatory and industry frameworks differ by service and jurisdiction.

01 · ASSESSMENT SELECTION

Different threats require different evidence.

ILI, pressure testing, direct assessment, direct examination, NDE, and other methods answer different questions. The first discipline is matching the assessment method to the threat, the pipeline configuration, the information required, and the limitations of the method.

IN-LINE INSPECTION

Use the tool that can characterize the threat.

Tool technology, sizing capability, detection limits, inspection conditions, prior run history, validation results, and data quality determine what confidence can reasonably be placed in an ILI result.

PRESSURE TESTING

A pressure test answers a different integrity question.

Hydrostatic and other pressure-test approaches can demonstrate pressure resistance and expose certain defects, but they do not provide the same location-specific condition information as inspection or direct examination.

DIRECT ASSESSMENT

Use a threat-specific pathway when the method is appropriate.

ECDA, ICDA, and SCCDA are structured processes that integrate pre-assessment, indirect evidence, targeted examination, and post-assessment learning for specific threats.

02 · DIRECT ASSESSMENT

The letters aren’t the point. The mechanism is.

ECDA, ICDA, and SCCDA follow a similar four-part structure: pre-assessment, indirect evaluation, direct examination, and post-assessment. That similarity can make them look interchangeable. They are not.

Each exists because a different damage mechanism behaves differently, develops under different conditions, and leaves different evidence.

Same framework. Different physics. Using the wrong assessment pathway can produce a technically complete process while still examining the wrong threat or the wrong location.

ECDA · EXTERNAL CORROSION

Where is external corrosion most likely, and what should be excavated?

ECDA integrates pipeline history, corrosion-control information, coating and environmental context, complementary above-ground inspection methods, excavation findings, and post-assessment learning. Under 49 CFR 192.925, ECDA is a four-step process: pre-assessment, indirect inspection, direct examination, and post-assessment.

Decision focus: identify and prioritize indications, determine excavation urgency, compare indirect evidence with actual pipe and coating condition, and use the findings to improve reassessment and corrosion-control decisions.

Why CP matters here: CP performance, coating condition, interference, electrical continuity, and field measurement quality can materially affect the evidence used in an ECDA program.

ICDA · INTERNAL CORROSION

Where can electrolyte collect inside the pipe, and where should the operator look first?

ICDA uses pipeline-specific operating, elevation, flow, geometry, and other data to identify locations where liquid or electrolyte may reside and internal corrosion is most likely. For dry-gas ICDA under 49 CFR 192.927, the process includes pre-assessment, indirect inspection, detailed examination, and post-assessment monitoring.

Decision focus: determine whether ICDA is feasible, define ICDA regions, predict likely liquid-holdup locations, examine the pipe at the locations most likely to contain corrosion, and validate the model against what is actually found.

Important boundary: the federal dry-gas ICDA process applies to specific gas-pipeline conditions and should not be treated as a universal internal-corrosion assessment method for every pipeline system.

49 CFR 192.927 · ICDA
SCCDA · STRESS CORROSION CRACKING

Do the operating history and environment support SCC susceptibility, and where should direct examination be concentrated?

SCCDA systematically integrates data about pipe, coating, cathodic protection, environment, operating stress, temperature, cyclic loading, and other susceptibility factors, then uses indirect surveys and direct examination to test whether SCC is present.

Decision focus: identify susceptible segments, prioritize locations for examination, characterize discovered cracking, determine remediation, and establish reassessment based on the mechanism and conditions found.

Important boundary: SCCDA is complementary to other SCC assessment methods such as ILI or hydrostatic testing with a spike test; it is not automatically a substitute for them.

49 CFR 192.929 · SCCDA
The practical distinction: ECDA follows evidence of external corrosion. ICDA follows the conditions that can create internal corrosion. SCCDA follows susceptibility and evidence associated with stress-corrosion cracking. The common discipline is to predict, examine, compare, learn, and decide.

03 · FIELD VALIDATION

From indication to observed condition.

Integrity excavations and direct examination are where an assessment prediction meets the physical asset. NDE, coating observations, measurements, photographs, samples, metallurgical or laboratory work, and repair records can confirm, refine, or contradict what the assessment suggested.

BEFORE THE DIG

Carry the assessment logic into the field.

The crew and technical team need to know what indication is being investigated, where it is expected, what measurements are required, and what evidence must be preserved.

AT THE PIPE

Document what is actually there.

Observed coating condition, corrosion morphology, crack characteristics, dimensions, NDE results, environmental observations, and photographs become part of the decision record.

AFTER THE DIG

Reconcile prediction with reality.

The value of direct examination is not limited to the repair. The finding should inform tool performance, prioritization logic, threat understanding, future assessment, and the asset record.

04 · DATA, RISK & SOFTWARE

The next decision depends on what the organization remembers.

Assessment results, field findings, repairs, operating history, corrosion-control information, material properties, and prior decisions become more useful when they remain connected and traceable over time. Integrity software can support that continuity. Risk models and AI can extend analysis, but neither replaces reliable source data, context, validation, or qualified judgment.

Assessment findings should remain connected to what was found in the field, what action was taken, how the asset record was updated, and what that information means for the next integrity decision.

TRACEABILITY

Keep the reasoning attached to the evidence.

Asset identity, location, date, method, tool or instrument, operating condition, units, assumptions, uncertainty, findings, and disposition are what make a record useful later.

DECISION SUPPORT

Software should make evidence easier to compare, not harder to understand.

Useful systems connect history, inspection, field evidence, remediation, risk, and follow-up so the next reviewer can understand what changed, what was decided, and why.

THE DECISION TEST

Did the assessment lead to better knowledge of the asset?

A credible integrity process should leave the organization with more than a completed inspection, test, excavation, or repair. It should leave a stronger understanding of the threat, better evidence about asset condition, a clear record of the action taken, and better information for the next decision.
